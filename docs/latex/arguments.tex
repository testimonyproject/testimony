\subsection*{Arguments.BornInBethlehem — Phase 1 vertical slice}
Matthew 2:5–6 quotes Micah 5:2 as grounds that the Messiah is born in Bethlehem. Both the Christian predictive reading and a critical reading are encoded, and the argument is carried through to a \texttt{Satisfaction\allowbreak{}Witness} — the first end-to-end connection from cited premises to a claim that a person meets a messianic criterion.

\texttt{jesus\allowbreak{}Born\allowbreak{}In\allowbreak{}Bethlehem} is cited to scripture alone. That is visible in the generated manifest via \texttt{scripture\allowbreak{}Only\allowbreak{}Atoms}, and it should be: the historicity of the Bethlehem birth is disputed in critical scholarship, and an argument that assumes it on the authority of the texts whose reading is in question is assuming part of what it sets out to show.

The argument is short enough to stay in one file. It runs on a \textbf{single line of reason}, which is exactly why it is the weakest of the fulfilment arguments and why \texttt{critical\_\allowbreak{}not\_\allowbreak{}establishes} costs the rival so little: deny the one interpretive premise and nothing is left standing. Compare \texttt{Born\allowbreak{}Of\allowbreak{}AVirgin}, which runs on four.

\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Born of a virgin — Isaiah 7:14, Genesis 3:15, Micah 5:2–3}

\subsection*{Arguments.BornOfAVirgin — Isaiah 7:14, Genesis 3:15, Micah 5:2–3}
Matthew 1:22–23 quotes Isaiah 7:14 as grounds that the Messiah is born of a virgin. Unlike Micah 5:2 (see \texttt{Born\allowbreak{}In\allowbreak{}Bethlehem}), the predictive reading here is heavily disputed, and the dispute is lexical: the Hebrew עַלְמָה (\emph{almah}) means "young woman" and does not by itself mean "virgin", while the Septuagint's παρθένος does. Isaiah's immediate context — a sign to Ahaz, apparently fulfilled within the prophet's own generation (cf. Isa 8:3–4) — is read by most critical and much Jewish scholarship as having nothing to do with a future Messiah.

The argument now runs on \textbf{four scriptural strands}, and that changes its shape. Each is encoded as a \texttt{Line} — its own grounds, its own inference step, what it delivers — and the packages are assembled from those rather than from flat premise lists. \texttt{Lines.lean} is where the shape of the argument is legible in one screen.

\textbf{The Isaianic strand} is the one above, hinging on \emph{almah}.

\textbf{The protoevangelium strand} reads Genesis 3:15 — enmity between the serpent's seed and "her seed", זֶרַע הָאִשָּׁה — as the first promise of a redeemer, and takes the matrilineal wording to mark a birth with no human father, since Hebrew ordinarily reckons זֶרַע through the man. That last step is genuinely contestable and is marked \texttt{disputed}: Genesis itself speaks of a woman's seed in wholly ordinary contexts (Gen 16:10 to Hagar, 24:60 to Rebekah), so the wording at 3:15 is not the anomaly the argument needs it to be unless the reader already grants the messianic reading.

\textbf{The Michean strand} reads Micah 5:2–3 — "until the time when she who is in labour has given birth" — as naming a mother and no father for the coming ruler. The objection is immediate and is encoded as the rival: this is an argument from silence, and an oracle about a woman in labour has no occasion to mention a father.

\textbf{The referential strand answers the lexical dispute without entering it.} \texttt{almah} need not \emph{mean} "virgin" for Mary to \emph{be} an \texttt{almah}. Genesis settles the compatibility outright: Rebekah is an עַלְמָה at 24:43 and a בְּתוּלָה "whom no man had known" at 24:16, the same woman under both terms. Mary was a betrothed young woman of marriageable age and conceived as a virgin, so she answers Isaiah's description on anyone's lexicon — and \texttt{semantic\_\allowbreak{}establishes} shows that Matthew's claim therefore never required the sense the objection demands.

That reply has a price, and \texttt{compatibility\_\allowbreak{}does\_\allowbreak{}not\_\allowbreak{}establish\_\allowbreak{}criterion} names it: the argument is purely defensive. It defeats the objection without delivering the criterion, which still has to come from one of the four scriptural strands. What it buys is that \emph{losing} the lexical dispute no longer costs the fulfilment claim.

\textbf{The versional evidence is encoded in full, including the half that tells against the reading.} Targum Jonathan renders עוּלֵימְתָא, "young woman", and does not read 7:14 messianically though it reads 9:5–6 and 11:1 so; Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion render νεᾶνις. Against them, the pre-Christian Septuagint renders παρθένος and the Syriac Peshitta ܒܬܘܠܬܐ, both "virgin" — so the Aramaic evidence is itself split, Jewish against Christian. 1QIsaᵃ confirms עַלְמָה in the Hebrew, so no textual variant rescues either side; the dispute is purely semantic. The usual Christian reply, that the Three revised against Christian use, is recorded but not leaned on: Theodotion's version is arguably pre-Christian, and the module says so rather than passing over it.

\texttt{compatibility\_\allowbreak{}defeats\_\allowbreak{}lexical\_\allowbreak{}objection} is where the tension pays off. Granting every versional datum and adding the referential premises, the objection no longer reaches its conclusion — because once virginity is compatible with the word, four translators making four different choices about how much of the referent to make explicit is not evidence about the sense.

\textbf{Wegner's objection is encoded in its sharpest form, and the module shows the circle in it rather than alleging one.} Wegner does not merely say that עַלְמָה means "young woman". He says that הָרָה at Isaiah 7:14 is a predicate adjective, so the clause reads "the עַלְמָה \emph{is} pregnant" — already, when the sign is given — and a pregnant woman is not a virgin; the one clear Isaianic case then settles the word. \texttt{wegner\_\allowbreak{}establishes} grants him every premise and checks that the argument goes through, because a rival worth answering has to be stated at full strength first.

The parse is not the weak point, and the encoding says so by granting it throughout: Rydelnik — cited here through Postell, who reports him at 474 — reads the same predicate adjective and draws the opposite conclusion, that the virgin \emph{is} pregnant and the sign is therefore as deep as Sheol. What the grammar does not supply is that the pregnancy is an \emph{ordinary} one, and that is the premise that excludes virginity. \texttt{wegner\_\allowbreak{}needs\_\allowbreak{}ordinary\_\allowbreak{}pregnancy} removes it and the objection stops. \texttt{reading\allowbreak{}Supplies\allowbreak{}Ordinary\allowbreak{}Pregnancy} then supplies it the way Wegner's own case does — from the near-term reading of the sign — and with the lexical conclusion turned back against that reading, the two legs close. \texttt{circle\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}lexical\_\allowbreak{}conclusion\_\allowbreak{}open} and \texttt{circle\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}denial\_\allowbreak{}open} are the result: keep every uncontested datum, and \emph{neither} end is settled — not established, and not refuted either. A cycle of implications is satisfied outright by a valuation on which all of its nodes are false, and equally by one granting Wegner's own conclusion. Nothing enters the loop, so nothing comes out of it, in either direction. That is a demonstration rather than a complaint, and it is conditional in the usual way: a defender of Wegner should deny \texttt{reading\allowbreak{}Supplies\allowbreak{}Ordinary\allowbreak{}Pregnancy} and argue that the near-term reading stands on the historical evidence alone.

Postell's second reply is independent of the first and survives that defence. The other clear עַלְמָה passages Wegner himself lists are cases of virgins, so a single referent cannot be what settles the denotation — the same principle would settle it the other way. \texttt{usage\_\allowbreak{}parity\_\allowbreak{}blocks\_\allowbreak{}wegner} checks it, and the atom is marked \texttt{plausible} rather than \texttt{well\allowbreak{}Supported} because Postell's "in fact virgins" is stronger than Wegner's own discussion of Song 6:8.

The critical case against the Isaianic strand is now \emph{derived} rather than assumed. \texttt{critical} used to carry \texttt{¬isaiah\allowbreak{}Predicts\allowbreak{}Virgin\allowbreak{}Birth} as a bare premise; it now carries the step that produces it — that a sign given for Ahaz's generation is not also a prediction of a virgin conception — and \texttt{critical\allowbreak{}Denial\_\allowbreak{}establishes} checks that the step works. Berry supplies the objection: how Isaiah 7:14 was fulfilled in Ahaz's own day is itself an open question, and \texttt{berry\_\allowbreak{}blocks\_\allowbreak{}critical\_\allowbreak{}denial} shows that granting him that much takes the exclusion out. It does no more than that. It does not establish the predictive reading and it does not touch the lexical dispute.

Berry also supplies a \emph{defensive} lexical premise — that the semantic range of עַלְמָה does not exclude "virgin" — and \texttt{admissibility\_\allowbreak{}is\_\allowbreak{}not\_\allowbreak{}enough} measures what it is worth: nothing, on its own. \emph{May mean} is not \emph{does mean}, and the whole Isaianic strand lives in that gap.

\textbf{The compositional strand} (Postell) declines the lexical fight altogether. Isaiah 7 sits inside Isaiah 2–12, a unit framed by eschatological vision at 2:1–4 and 11:1–12:6; on the principle that an oracle's meaning in a finished book is set by its literary placement rather than by the events it describes — argued from the making of canonical Jeremiah — 7:14 anticipates a future, miraculous birth. The hinge is \texttt{composition\allowbreak{}Governs\allowbreak{}Meaning}, and a reader who holds that an oracle's historical setting fixes its sense rejects it outright.

Postell also supplies the module's sharpest rival-defeater. \texttt{parity\allowbreak{}Defeats} \texttt{Near\allowbreak{}Term\allowbreak{}Exclusion} observes that Isaiah 9:5–6 and 11:1–10 are read as messianic without reservation \emph{and} stand on the same near-term Assyrian timeline as 7:14 — the invasion of 8:7–8, the oppressor's rod of 10:5. So a near-term setting cannot be what rules out messianic reference, or it would rule out 9 and 11 too. \texttt{parity\_\allowbreak{}blocks\_\allowbreak{}critical\_\allowbreak{}denial} checks it. Notably the Targum is cited \emph{for} this premise: it reads 9:5–6 and 11:1 messianically while declining to read 7:14 so, which makes it a hostile witness and therefore a strong one.

That gives the critical exclusion step two independent defeaters. Berry's is evidential — we do not know how the sign was fulfilled in Ahaz's day. Postell's is structural — the near-term setting was never the right kind of reason.

\textbf{Taken together, the positions make a dispute, and the dispute has an outcome the entailment results cannot state.} \texttt{Dispute.lean} treats the scriptural reading, the critical denial, Berry, Postell and Motyer as the nodes of an argumentation framework and proves who defeats whom — a defeat being an attack derived from entailment that the cited confidences do not block. Against the scriptural reading alone, \texttt{nothing\_\allowbreak{}prevails\_\allowbreak{}unanswered}: the critical denial and the scriptural reading defeat each other, because the weakest premise on each side is \texttt{disputed} (\texttt{christian\_\allowbreak{}defeats\_\allowbreak{}critical}). Hear Berry and Postell, and \texttt{scriptural\_\allowbreak{}reading\_\allowbreak{}prevails\_\allowbreak{}once\_\allowbreak{}replies\_\allowbreak{}are\_\allowbreak{}heard}: nothing defeats Postell, he defeats the critic, and so he defends the scriptural reading — and Berry, and Motyer with Compton — against the only party that defeats them. The critic is left with no defence at all (\texttt{critical\_\allowbreak{}denial\_\allowbreak{}indefensible}). Inference steps are rated like premises, and Berry's and Motyer's are \texttt{disputed}, so alone they tie with the critic: \texttt{nothing\_\allowbreak{}prevails\_\allowbreak{}on\_\allowbreak{}motyer\_\allowbreak{}alone}, and \texttt{motyer\_\allowbreak{}rests\_\allowbreak{}on\_\allowbreak{}his\_\allowbreak{}inference} shows the weight of his reply is on that step. The outcome turns on Postell's inference being \texttt{plausible} — no cited source grants his grounds and keeps the exclusion — and without him nothing prevails (\texttt{nothing\_\allowbreak{}prevails\_\allowbreak{}without\_\allowbreak{}postell}). It turns too on the critic's premises being \texttt{disputed}, as the replies, Motyer and Compton contest them.

\textbf{Luke is a second witness to the conception, not to the prophecy.} His account is parallel to Matthew's and independent of it, but it never quotes Isaiah 7:14. Luke 1:31 is close to the Septuagint's wording, and whether that is an allusion is disputed: \texttt{luke\allowbreak{}Allusion\allowbreak{}Edge} records it as one, attributed, and \texttt{annunciation\allowbreak{}Form\allowbreak{}Edge} records the rival reading, on which the wording is the stock birth-announcement form that Isaiah 7:14 itself uses. Luke adds to \texttt{independent\allowbreak{}Attestation}; he adds nothing to the rating of the predictive reading.

\texttt{hinges\_\allowbreak{}jointly\_\allowbreak{}load\_\allowbreak{}bearing} is the result worth having. No single interpretive hinge carries the argument any more — strip \emph{almah} and the other three strands still deliver the criterion, which \texttt{almah\_\allowbreak{}not\_\allowbreak{}load\_\allowbreak{}bearing} records. Strip all four and the argument collapses. \texttt{almah\_\allowbreak{}is\_\allowbreak{}load\_\allowbreak{}bearing\_\allowbreak{}alone} keeps the pre-Miravalle finding on the books: within the Isaianic strand taken by itself, the lexical premise still carries everything.

\textbf{The magisterial strand is encoded separately and is not part of \texttt{christian}.} Papal teaching on Mary's virginity in conceiving, as collated by Miravalle, is a Roman Catholic premise; this library's author does not grant that magisterial teaching settles doctrinal questions, and the encoding says so rather than quietly folding it in. \texttt{catholic} is the package for a reader who does grant it. \texttt{magisterial\_\allowbreak{}authority\_\allowbreak{}is\_\allowbreak{}load\_\allowbreak{}bearing} shows what follows for a reader who does not: that route yields nothing on its own, and the four scriptural strands have to carry the argument by themselves. The rival is cited to Westminster I.x.

Matthew's and Luke's birth narratives agree on the virgin conception while differing in nearly every other detail, and that convergence is encoded as a multiple-attestation premise — though the independence of the two traditions is itself debated, and is marked \texttt{plausible} rather than \texttt{well\allowbreak{}Supported}.

\subsection*{Arguments.BornOfAVirgin.Lines — the steps, and the lines of reason}
Every inference this argument makes, and the four strands they compose into.

Inference steps carry no \texttt{confidence} field of their own — a step is a claim that \emph{these premises license that conclusion}, which is a different kind of claim from an atom, and the library does not pretend to grade it. Where a step is contestable even though the atoms it joins are well supported, the docstring says so; \texttt{berry\allowbreak{}Blocks\allowbreak{}Exclusion} is the clearest case.

\paragraph*{Inference steps}
\leavevmode\par
One per strand, each delivering the same criterion, plus the steps that close the argument and the steps the rivals need. Keeping them separate is what makes the load-bearing results possible: a strand can be removed without touching the others.

\[(P_{28} \land P_{29} \land P_{30} \land P_{31}) \rightarrow P_{46}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{compositional\allowbreak{}To\allowbreak{}Criterion}}\par\nobreak\noindent
Compositional strand: from Isaiah 7's placement in an eschatologically framed unit, the principle that placement governs meaning, and the future birth that follows, the criterion follows.

The weak joint is the last step. Postell's argument delivers a \emph{miraculous} birth; it is \texttt{matthew\allowbreak{}Intends\allowbreak{}Fulfilment} that says which miracle. A reader who grants the composition and stops short of Matthew is entitled to.

\[(P_{23} \land P_{24} \land P_{27} \land P_{22}) \rightarrow P_{46}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{parity\allowbreak{}Defeats\allowbreak{}Near\allowbreak{}Term\allowbreak{}Exclusion}}\par\nobreak\noindent
\textbf{Postell's parity argument.} Isaiah 9:5–6 and 11:1–10 are read as messianic without reservation, and they stand on the same near-term Assyrian timeline as 7:14 — the Assyrian invasion of 8:7–8, the oppressor's rod of 10:5. So a near-term geopolitical setting cannot be what rules out messianic reference, because if it were it would rule out 9 and 11 as well.

This is a second, independent defeater of the same critical step that Berry's objection attacks, and it is the stronger of the two: Berry says the near-term fulfilment is unclear, while parity says the near-term setting was never the right kind of reason.

\[(P_{25} \land P_{26}) \rightarrow \lnot P_{37}\]

\[(P_{32} \land P_{33}) \rightarrow P_{46}\]

\[(P_{43} \land P_{44}) \rightarrow P_{46}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{critical\allowbreak{}Exclusion}}\par\nobreak\noindent
The critical inference, made explicit: granted that Isaiah 7:14 was a sign to Ahaz, and that such a sign is not also a prediction of a virgin conception, the predictive reading is denied.

The earlier encoding of this module simply asserted the denial as a premise of \texttt{critical}. That was a weaker rival than the critical case actually is, and it left nothing for an objection to engage.

\[(P_{36} \land P_{37}) \rightarrow \lnot P_{1}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{berry\allowbreak{}Blocks\allowbreak{}Exclusion}}\par\nobreak\noindent
Berry's objection: if how the sign was fulfilled in Ahaz's own day is itself unsettled, the near-term reading is not secure enough to exclude a further referent.

The step is contestable even though both atoms it joins are \texttt{well\allowbreak{}Supported} — an unclear fulfilment is still a fulfilment, and Brown would answer that Isaiah 8:3–4 settles the referent well enough. \[P_{38} \rightarrow \lnot P_{37}\]

Motyer's inference: the sign of 7:14 is given to the dynasty, and the timetable that answers Ahaz's crisis passes to Maher-shalal-hash-baz, so the child of 7:14 is not a near-term sign to Ahaz. This is the contested part of his reply, and it is a step rather than an atom on purpose, as Berry's and Postell's are: the two atoms it joins are what the text says, and the inference is what a reader who identifies the two children denies, and \texttt{motyer\allowbreak{}Line} rates it \texttt{disputed} for that reason.

\paragraph*{The referential strand}
\leavevmode\par
Sense against reference. The steps below never claim that עַלְמָה \emph{means} virgin; they claim that a virgin \emph{is} an עַלְמָה, which is a different and far cheaper claim, and then ask what the lexical objection is left with.

\paragraph*{Wegner's grammatical objection, and the circle Postell finds in it}
\leavevmode\par
The sharpest form of the lexical case. Each link is a step of its own, because the result worth having is not whether the objection is valid — it is — but which link carries its weight, and where that link comes from.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{harah\allowbreak{}Yields\allowbreak{}Present\allowbreak{}Pregnancy}}\par\nobreak\noindent
Wegner's grammatical step: if הָרָה is a predicate adjective, the עַלְמָה of the sign is pregnant already, at the moment the sign is given.

\[P_{15} \rightarrow P_{16}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{ordinary\allowbreak{}Pregnancy\allowbreak{}Excludes\allowbreak{}Virginity}}\par\nobreak\noindent
... and if that pregnancy is an ordinary conception, the woman it describes is not a virgin.

The second conjunct does all the work, and it is not a grammatical claim. Rydelnik parses הָרָה exactly as Wegner does and reads the same clause as the miracle itself — the virgin \emph{is} pregnant — which is available to him precisely because the parse leaves this open.

\[(P_{16} \land P_{17}) \rightarrow P_{18}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{referent\allowbreak{}Yields\allowbreak{}Lexical\allowbreak{}Conclusion}}\par\nobreak\noindent

\[(P_{18} \land P_{19}) \rightarrow \lnot P_{2}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{reading\allowbreak{}Supplies\allowbreak{}Ordinary\allowbreak{}Pregnancy}}\par\nobreak\noindent
\textbf{Where the ordinary pregnancy comes from.} Not from the grammar: from the near-term reading of the sign, which has the child born within nine months and the whole oracle discharged by 701 BC.

This is Postell's charge, written as a formula rather than repeated as a complaint: "because Wegner rejects the messianic interpretation on grammatical-historical grounds, he assumes that עַלְמָה cannot mean 'virgin' in Isaiah 7:14" (468 n. 22).

\[(P_{36} \land \lnot P_{1}) \rightarrow P_{17}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{lexical\allowbreak{}Conclusion\allowbreak{}Tells\allowbreak{}Against\allowbreak{}Prediction}}\par\nobreak\noindent
\textbf{The return leg.} The lexical conclusion is then turned against the predictive reading, which is what the critical case does with it and what \texttt{critical} already assumes.

Wegner's own version of this step is weaker — Postell reports it as decreasing the likelihood of a virgin-birth prediction without ruling it out (469) — and the encoding states the strong form on purpose. The strong form is the one on which the two legs make a closed circle, and stating it is what lets \texttt{circle\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}lexical\_\allowbreak{}conclusion\_\allowbreak{}open} be checked rather than asserted. On the weaker form the loop is evidential rather than deductive, which is the difference between a vicious circle and mutual support.

\[(\lnot P_{2} \land P_{8}) \rightarrow \lnot P_{1}\]

\subsection*{Arguments.BornOfAVirgin.Packages — the positions, and the variants}
A handful of these packages are positions someone holds; the rest exist to be \emph{refuted}, because "this premise is load-bearing" is proved by removing it and exhibiting a countermodel, and the package with it removed has to be written down to be refuted. So the file grows with every result, and counting it here would only be one more thing to keep current.

Each variant is a named difference from \texttt{christian}: one line on different grounds, or one fewer line. Before \texttt{Line} existed each restated the whole twenty-one element premise list, where a reader could not see which premise had gone and a slip in the other twenty was invisible.

\subsection*{Arguments.BornOfAVirgin.Results — what does and does not follow}
Every result the module claims, each tagged \texttt{@[headline]} and each followed by \texttt{\#print axioms} so its trust base is visible in the source.

Entailments are established with \texttt{establish}; refutations name a countermodel and use \texttt{refute\_\allowbreak{}with}. A countermodel here is the rival's reading written down as a valuation, which is why each one is a named definition with a docstring saying what position it encodes rather than an anonymous function.

\subsection*{Arguments.BornOfAVirgin.Dispute — who prevails over Isaiah 7:14}
The results in \texttt{Results.lean} ask what each package entails. This module asks what happens when the packages meet: the scriptural reading, the critical denial of the predictive reading, and three replies to that denial — Berry's objection, Postell's parity argument, and Motyer's reply, with Compton's — taken together as one dispute.

Who defeats whom is not stipulated. Each defeat below is a theorem about the packages' premises, and so is each absence of one (see \texttt{Testimony.Logic.Dispute} for how an attack is derived from entailment and filtered by cited confidence). The defeats, all four proved, are these:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The critical denial defeats the scriptural reading.} It entails the
\end{itemize}
  negation of the scriptural premise that Isaiah 7:14 predicts a virgin birth,   a premise cited as \texttt{disputed}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The scriptural reading defeats the critical denial back.} It undermines
\end{itemize}
  none of the critic's premises, but it concludes the opposite, so it rebuts;   and the weakest link on each side is \texttt{disputed}, so neither outranks the   other.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Berry and Postell each defeat the critical denial}, by contradicting its
\end{itemize}
  premise that a near-term sign excludes a messianic sense, cited \texttt{disputed}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Motyer defeats it too}, by contradicting its other premise: that 7:14 is
\end{itemize}
  a near-term sign to Ahaz at all, also cited \texttt{disputed}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The critical denial defeats Berry and Motyer back, but not Postell.} It
\end{itemize}
  rebuts all three. Berry's and Motyer's inferences are contested — a cited   source grants each one's grounds and denies its conclusion — so they are no   stronger than the critic. Postell's inference is not, and he outranks it.

\subsubsection*{What follows}
Faced with the scriptural reading alone, \textbf{nothing prevails}: the two defeat each other, and the grounded extension is empty. Add the replies and \textbf{the scriptural reading prevails}. Nothing defeats Postell; he defeats the critic, which is the only party that defeats the scriptural reading, Berry or Motyer; and so all four stand. The critical denial belongs to no admissible set at all, because nothing answers Postell. Take Postell away and nothing prevails again.

This is the result entailment could not state. Adding premises never removes a conclusion; adding arguments can change what a dispute forces, and here it turns an open question into a settled one. The replies do not show that the predictive reading is true. They show that, on these premises and these ratings, the case against it does not survive them.

\subsubsection*{What the ratings decide}
These outcomes rest on the cited confidences as much as on the premises, and the inferences are rated as the premises are: \texttt{disputed} when a cited source grants a step's grounds and denies its conclusion. Before they were rated, a reply that put its contested move in a step was weighed by its observations alone, and every reply outranked the critic. Rated, only one does.

The critic's two premises are \texttt{disputed}: Berry, Postell and Motyer contest the exclusion, Motyer and Compton the near-term sign. Its inference is \texttt{consensus}, because it only applies the exclusion to 7:14.

Berry's inference is \texttt{disputed}. Watts grants that the chronology is uncertain and still reads 7:14 of Hezekiah, as Compton reports. Motyer's is \texttt{disputed} for the same reason: those who identify the two children grant both his observations and keep the near-term sign, and \texttt{motyer\_\allowbreak{}rests\_\allowbreak{}on\_\allowbreak{}his\_\allowbreak{}inference} proves that the whole weight of his reply falls on that step. So each ties with the critic, and heard alone Motyer settles nothing (\texttt{nothing\_\allowbreak{}prevails\_\allowbreak{}on\_\allowbreak{}motyer\_\allowbreak{}alone}).

Postell's inference is \texttt{plausible}. It is a counterexample to the exclusion — two oracles on the same Assyrian timeline, read messianically — and no source cited here grants his grounds and keeps the exclusion. \textbf{The verdict rests on that rating.} Without Postell, every party is defeated by someone and nothing prevails (\texttt{nothing\_\allowbreak{}prevails\_\allowbreak{}without\_\allowbreak{}postell}); and rated \texttt{disputed}, his inference would tie him with the critic as Berry's and Motyer's do. A critic who granted that Isaiah 9 and 11 are messianic on the Assyrian timeline, and still held the exclusion, would be the source to find. Postell's argument is described in \texttt{Lines.lean} as the stronger of the two; the ratings now register that, and it is what decides the dispute.

\subsubsection*{What the church fathers add, and what they cannot}
The predictive reading is not a modern apologetic. Justin, Irenaeus, Origen and Jerome all argue it, and \texttt{Sources.lean} cites them for it: for the prediction itself, for עַלְמָה as a virgin, and — the argument they share — that an ordinary conception would have been no sign at all. That strengthens the attestation of the scriptural premises, and none of it changes a rating.

It cannot, because of what \texttt{disputed} means here: \emph{actively contested by competent scholars}. The fathers are the earliest witnesses to the contest as well as to the reading. Justin records Trypho's answer — "young woman", and fulfilled in Hezekiah — and Irenaeus names Theodotion and Aquila. An older witness does not make a contested claim uncontested. What moved the result was the same definition applied to the other side: both of the critic's premises are contested too, and the library records who contests them.

\subsection*{Arguments.SolaFide — justification by grace through faith, not works}
The Reformation's material principle, encoded so that its premises are visible and its disputed step is identified mechanically.

The argument runs on \textbf{two independent strands}, and that is the point.

\textbf{The Pauline strand} turns on \texttt{works\allowbreak{}Of\allowbreak{}Law\allowbreak{}Means\allowbreak{}Works\allowbreak{}Generally}: whether Paul's ἔργα νόμου denotes human works in general, or specifically the covenant boundary markers — circumcision, food laws, sabbath — that marked Jews off from gentiles, as Dunn and Wright argue. This is the most contested step in the traditional case.

\textbf{The dominical strand} rests on Jesus' own words rather than Paul's. ἡ πίστις σου σέσωκέν σε — "your faith has saved you" — occurs four times in Luke (7:50, 8:48, 17:19, 18:42). It is not a free win: σῴζω means both \emph{save} and \emph{heal}, and in 8:48, 17:19 and 18:42 the context is physical healing, which is why most translations render "made you well" there. The argument therefore rests on \textbf{Luke 7:50}, where the saying follows "your sins are forgiven" rather than a healing, and carries \texttt{sozo\allowbreak{}Is\allowbreak{}Soteriological} as its own disputed premise. It is a second bet, on a different word.

Encoding both strands yields a result the single-strand version could not: neither lexical premise is load-bearing on its own, because either strand carries the conclusion without the other. Only their \emph{disjunction} is load-bearing. A reader who wins the ἔργα νόμου argument outright does not thereby defeat sola fide — and the New Perspective, which accepts justification by faith while rejecting the traditional reading of Paul's phrase, turns out to establish the conclusion too.

\textbf{James 2:24} — "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone", the only occurrence of \emph{faith alone} in the New Testament — is no longer assumed away. Its compatibility with Paul is now \emph{derived} from two cited premises: that James's target is a barren faith, mere assent ("even the demons believe", Jas 2:19), and that works are the fruit and evidence of saving faith rather than a ground of justification. Moo and Johnson for the exegesis; Westminster XI.2 for the confessional form — faith "is not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces, and is no dead faith, but worketh by love". \texttt{james\_\allowbreak{}harmonisation\_\allowbreak{}is\_\allowbreak{}load\_\allowbreak{}bearing} shows the argument genuinely depends on this: drop it and sola fide does not follow.

\subsection*{Arguments.SolaFide.Lines — the strands, and what they share}
Two strands deliver justification by faith alone, by different words in different authors. A third line answers James. What every package holds in common is collected here too, so that a variant package is a named difference rather than a retyped list.

\paragraph*{Lines of reason}
\leavevmode\par
Two strands deliver the conclusion, and a third line answers James. The James harmonisation is deliberately \emph{not} a strand: it does not reach \texttt{justification\allowbreak{}By\allowbreak{}Faith\allowbreak{}Alone} by an independent route, it supplies a premise the closing step needs. So it contributes its grounds and its step to the case without being one of the lines \texttt{case\allowbreak{}Of} treats as converging.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{pauline\allowbreak{}To\allowbreak{}Faith\allowbreak{}Alone}}\par\nobreak\noindent
\textbf{The Pauline strand.} From Romans and Galatians to justification by faith alone, by way of the disputed lexical premise about ἔργα νόμου.

\[(P_{2} \land P_{3} \land P_{6}) \rightarrow P_{13}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{dominical\allowbreak{}To\allowbreak{}Faith\allowbreak{}Alone}}\par\nobreak\noindent
\textbf{The dominical strand.} From Jesus' own words at Luke 7:50 to justification by faith alone, by way of the disputed lexical premise about σῴζω. Independent of Paul, and of the ἔργα νόμου dispute.

\[(P_{7} \land P_{8}) \rightarrow P_{13}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{james\allowbreak{}Harmonisation}}\par\nobreak\noindent
The harmonisation of James: because James's target is barren faith, and works are the fruit of saving faith rather than its ground, James 2:24 does not contradict Paul. Derived rather than assumed.

\[(P_{9} \land P_{10}) \rightarrow P_{11}\]
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{to\allowbreak{}Salvation}}\par\nobreak\noindent
The inference from justification by faith alone to the full claim about salvation, requiring the remaining prooftexts and the harmonisation of James.

\[(P_{13} \land P_{1} \land P_{4} \land P_{5} \land P_{11} \land P_{12}) \rightarrow P_{14}\]

\subsection*{Arguments.SolaFide.Results — what does and does not follow}
Entailments are established with \texttt{establish}; refutations name a countermodel — the rival's own reading, written down as a valuation — and use \texttt{refute\_\allowbreak{}with}.

\texttt{lexical\_\allowbreak{}premises\_\allowbreak{}jointly\_\allowbreak{}load\_\allowbreak{}bearing} is the result the two-strand encoding exists to make possible.

\paragraph*{Results}
\leavevmode\par
Entailments are established with \texttt{tauto}; refutations name a countermodel — the rival's own reading, written down as a valuation.

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{reformed\_\allowbreak{}establishes}}\par\nobreak\noindent
Given the Reformed premises, the conclusion follows.

-- axioms: propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound
\end{verbatim}}
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{new\allowbreak{}Perspective\_\allowbreak{}establishes}}\par\nobreak\noindent
\textbf{The New Perspective establishes it too.} Dunn and Wright reject the traditional reading of ἔργα νόμου; they do not reject justification by faith. Once Jesus' words at Luke 7:50 are in view, denying the Pauline lexical premise no longer blocks the conclusion.

Winning the ἔργα νόμου argument outright is therefore not a defeat of sola fide, and this library says so in a form either side can check.

\paragraph*{Satisfiability}
\leavevmode\par
\texttt{Entails} is vacuously true over a premise set with no model, so a package built from contradictory premises would establish its conclusion and every gate would pass. Not tagged \texttt{@[headline]} — these are claims about the health of the encoding rather than about justification.

\subsection*{Arguments.SolaScriptura — scripture as the sole infallible rule of faith}
The formal principle of the Reformation. Sola fide is a claim \emph{within} scripture's teaching; sola scriptura is a claim \emph{about} scripture's authority, and so must answer objections the material principle never faces.

\subsubsection*{What the dispute is not about}
\textbf{It is not about whether doctrine develops.} Both Rome and the Protestant position encoded here hold that the deposit is closed while understanding of it deepens — \texttt{doctrine\allowbreak{}Develops\allowbreak{}Without\allowbreak{}New\allowbreak{}Revelation} is shared ground, held by Newman and by Mathison alike. The disagreement is entirely over what \emph{authenticates} a development: derivability from scripture, or the authority of the developing body. An encoding that opposed a static Protestantism to a developing Rome would describe a dispute neither party is having.

\subsubsection*{Four positions, not two}
Mathison's taxonomy, after Oberman, separates what the seed encoding collapsed:

\begin{longtable}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.45\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Position} & \textbf{What it holds} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{Tradition 0} & Tradition carries no binding authority; creeds inform without norming \\
\textbf{Tradition I} & Tradition is ministerial — real authority, fallible, subordinate to scripture \\
\textbf{Tradition II} & Two coordinate sources of revelation, canonised at Trent \\
\textbf{Tradition III} & The magisterium is the one real source, resting on Vatican I \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
Orthodoxy is a fifth thing again, and not a variant of Rome: authority rests in the mind of the Church, expressed in the consensus of the Fathers and in conciliar decisions received by the whole Church. Florovsky rejects the two-sources model outright. The seed grounded a single "Catholic/Orthodox" line on \texttt{magisterium\allowbreak{}Is\allowbreak{}Infallible}, which attributes to Orthodoxy a premise it does not hold.

\texttt{tradition0\_\allowbreak{}establishes} is the result that most repays the split: the position Mathison calls "solo scriptura" and judges unbiblical and unworkable reaches the conclusion too, once stated by Geisler — who holds it — rather than by its critic.

\subsubsection*{Three objections, which do not reduce to one another}
\textbf{Self-refutation.} If a doctrine binds only when scripture teaches it, and scripture does not teach sola scriptura, the position fails by its own standard.

\textbf{The canon objection.} If the canon is known through the Church's reception, an authority outside scripture is needed to identify scripture. Augustine supplies the classical form: \emph{ego vero Evangelio non crederem, nisi me catholicae Ecclesiae commoveret auctoritas}.

\textbf{The interpretive-authority regress.} Cross and Judisch argue that the individual retains ultimate interpretive authority, exercised indirectly by choosing which body to submit to, so Tradition I is not principled distinct from Tradition 0 — "when I submit (so long as I agree), the one to whom I submit is me."

Encoding objections that tell against the position is not a concession; the project's ground rules require rival readings to be encoded with the same care as the ones argued for.

\subsubsection*{The hinge does two jobs, and they come apart}
The seed asserted in prose that the dispute reduces to \texttt{sola\allowbreak{}Scriptura\allowbreak{}Is\allowbreak{}Taught\allowbreak{}By\allowbreak{}Scripture}. Split into the two roles that premise actually plays, the claim is both weaker and sharper than that.

It is \textbf{not} load-bearing for reaching the conclusion: \texttt{hinge\_\allowbreak{}not\_\allowbreak{}load\_\allowbreak{}bearing\_\allowbreak{}for\_\allowbreak{}conclusion} shows the eliminative line delivering the sole rule without it. It \textbf{is} load-bearing within the classical strand taken alone.

And against the self-refutation objection it is one of \emph{two} answers, not the only one. The objection's step is a conjunction, so either conjunct may be denied: \texttt{classical\_\allowbreak{}answer\_\allowbreak{}blocks\_\allowbreak{}self\_\allowbreak{}refutation} denies that scripture fails to teach the principle, and \texttt{scoping\_\allowbreak{}blocks\_\allowbreak{}self\_\allowbreak{}refutation} denies instead that the bindingness rule governs a claim about where binding doctrine comes from — the final-arbiter reading, on which scripture is not the only arbiter but the last one. The objection succeeds only against a position refusing both.

\textbf{Redundancy does not defend a position here}, which is where this argument parts company with \texttt{Sola\allowbreak{}Fide}. There, a second strand defeated an attack on a premise. These objections are not premise attacks: each independently delivers the negation of the conclusion, so a further route to the conclusion does nothing against them. What answers an objection is denying one of \emph{its} grounds.

\subsubsection*{The parity reply, and what it is worth}
Kruger on the canon and Mathison on interpretive authority make the same move: concede the circularity, deny that it discriminates between the positions. What the move achieves is that the disputed proposition comes out \emph{independent} of the reply's grounds: neither it nor its denial follows, because two readings satisfy those grounds and disagree about it. \texttt{parity\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}canon\_\allowbreak{}open} and \texttt{parity\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}distinction\_\allowbreak{}open} are those results.

Each used to be a \emph{pair} — one result that the reply blocks the objection, another that it establishes nothing — and the pair is what hid a defect. Over Barrett's parity reply to Geisler the two halves asked about different propositions while being presented as complements; \texttt{parity\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}defeat\_\allowbreak{}open} is the claim that was meant, and \texttt{circle\_\allowbreak{}parity\_\allowbreak{}concedes\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}charge} is the second question restated, since that reply does not merely fail to clear the charge of circularity but grants it as one of its own grounds.

The replies neutralise; they do not establish. \texttt{compatibility\_\allowbreak{}does\_\allowbreak{}not\_\allowbreak{}establish\_\allowbreak{}criterion} records the same lesson in \texttt{Born\allowbreak{}Of\allowbreak{}AVirgin}, for a different argument and a different reply — and \emph{not} as an independence claim, because its two results range over different premise sets.

\subsubsection*{The circle inside Protestantism}
\texttt{circle\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}consensus\_\allowbreak{}open} encodes Geisler's charge against Tradition I: the creedal consensus is said to rest on scripture's clarity, while scripture's clear sense is said to be unobtainable without that consensus. Both legs in place, neither end is settled either way — a cycle of implications is satisfied outright by a valuation on which every node in it is false, and equally by one on which every node is true. \texttt{circle\_\allowbreak{}leaves\_\allowbreak{}the\_\allowbreak{}perspicuity\_\allowbreak{}open} says the same of the other end.

That charge is made from inside the Reformation, against the position this module encodes as the Protestant one. It is conditional like every result here: deny either leg and the circle is not there.

\subsubsection*{A limitation worth stating}
The final-arbiter reading turns on a distinction between \textbf{doctrine}, which requires scriptural adjudication, and \textbf{practice} neither commanded nor forbidden, which does not. That is a \emph{scope} distinction, and this fragment is propositional: it cannot quantify. The distinction is carried by atom design and by this docstring, not by the logic. What the encoding shows is what follows given the distinction, not that the distinction can be drawn.

\subsection*{Arguments.SolaScriptura.Lines — the inference steps and the lines of reason}
Two lines reach the conclusion, three positions deny it, and three objections attack it from different directions. The objections do not reduce to one another: self-refutation says the doctrine fails its own standard, the canon objection says an authority outside scripture is needed to identify scripture, and the interpretive-authority regress says the authority claimed is the claimant in disguise.

\textbf{Redundancy does not defend the hinge here.} In \texttt{Sola\allowbreak{}Fide}, adding a second strand defeated an attack on a premise. These objections are not premise attacks: each independently delivers the negation of the conclusion, so a further route to the conclusion does nothing against them. What answers an objection is denying one of \emph{its} grounds, which is why every reply below is written with \texttt{Line.on\allowbreak{}Grounds} — the objection's own line, with the ground the reply refuses put back as what the reply offers instead.

\subsection*{Arguments.SolaScriptura.Packages — the positions, and the variants results refute}
Four positions on tradition, three objections, two parity replies, and the variants each result needs. The rivals were written before anything was proved about the position argued for.

Several packages are built to be \emph{refuted}. A reply is shown to block an objection by writing the objection's line on the reply's grounds and exhibiting a countermodel.

The parity replies have no packages at all. What they do is that the disputed proposition comes out \emph{independent} of the reply's grounds — neither entailed nor refuted by them — and \texttt{Independent} takes a premise list and a formula, so the reply's \texttt{Line} is all it needs.

\subsection*{Arguments.SolaScriptura.Results — what does and does not follow}
The results this module exists for are the two on the hinge. The seed asserted in prose that the dispute reduces to \texttt{sola\allowbreak{}Scriptura\allowbreak{}Is\allowbreak{}Taught\allowbreak{}By\allowbreak{}Scripture}; here that claim is split into the two roles the premise actually plays, because they come apart.

\paragraph*{The positions}

\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{protestant\_\allowbreak{}establishes}}\par\nobreak\noindent
Given the Protestant premises, on either route, the conclusion follows.

theorem protestant_establishes : Establishes protestant
-- axioms: propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound
\end{verbatim}}
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{tradition0\_\allowbreak{}establishes}}\par\nobreak\noindent
\textbf{Tradition 0 establishes it too.} Geisler rejects Mathison's claim that tradition is hermeneutically necessary; he does not reject sola scriptura. The position Mathison calls "solo scriptura" and judges unbiblical and unworkable reaches the same conclusion by a different route, once it is stated by someone who holds it rather than by its critic.

{\small\begin{verbatim}
theorem tradition0_establishes : Establishes tradition0
-- axioms: propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound
\end{verbatim}}
\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{\texttt{tridentine\allowbreak{}Reading}}\par\nobreak\noindent
The Tridentine reading, written down: tradition is a coordinate source, so scripture is not the sole rule.

{\small\begin{verbatim}
def tridentineReading : Valuation Claim :=
  fun a =>
    match a with
    | Claim.scriptureIsSoleInfallibleRule => False
    | x => True
\end{verbatim}}

\paragraph*{The parity replies, and what they are worth}
\leavevmode\par
Kruger on the canon and Mathison on interpretive authority make the same move: concede the circularity, deny that it discriminates between the positions.

Each used to get two results — one that it blocks the objection, one that it establishes nothing — and that is one result too many. A premise set that entails neither a proposition nor its negation leaves that proposition \emph{independent} of it, and blocking and establishing-nothing are the two halves of that single fact. Foundation names the notion for provability; \texttt{Testimony.Logic.Independent} is its semantic counterpart, and \texttt{leaves\_\allowbreak{}open} proves it from the two readings the premises admit.

The gain is not brevity. Stated as a pair, nothing checks that the two halves are about the same proposition — and in the Geisler case below they were not.

\paragraph*{Refusing a ground, and denying it}
\leavevmode\par
Every reply above is written the same way, as the objection's own line with a ground replaced. That shape covers two different moves. A reply can \textbf{deny} the ground — entail its negation — or merely \textbf{refuse} it, declining to grant it without asserting the opposite. A denial is an \emph{attack}, and in a dispute an attack can defeat (\texttt{Testimony.Logic.Dispute}). A refusal attacks nothing: it takes the ground away, which is what \texttt{Line.on\allowbreak{}Grounds} encodes, and the question it leaves is an entailment question the results above already answer.

Five of the seven replies here refuse. Each refusal is shown by a reading that grants the reply's grounds and keeps the refused ground true. The other two deny, and each denial is an entailment.

\paragraph*{Satisfiability}
\leavevmode\par
\texttt{Entails} quantifies over the valuations satisfying the premises, so a premise set with no model entails everything — including the negation of what its own author intended. These are not tagged \texttt{@[headline]}. They are claims about the health of the encoding, not about the dispute.
